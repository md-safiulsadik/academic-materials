Two‑Bit Adder — explained super simply

We’ll first understand a full adder (1‑bit), then chain two of them to make a two‑bit adder. You’ll also get an interactive demo and a real‑life analogy.

1) What’s a Full Adder (1‑bit)?

A full adder takes three 1‑bit inputs — A, B, and Cin (carry‑in) — and outputs a 1‑bit Sum and a 1‑bit Cout (carry‑out).

Logic (no scary math)

Sum is “odd count of 1s”: it becomes 1 if an odd number of inputs are 1. That’s exactly what XOR does.

Cout is 1 if at least two inputs are 1 (because adding two 1s makes a carry). That’s a simple “majority of three”.

Formulas

Sum = A ⊕ B ⊕ Cin

Cout = (A·B) + (A·Cin) + (B·Cin)

2) Building a Two‑Bit Adder from 2 Full Adders

We place two full adders in a row. The first adds the lowest bits (LSB), and its Cout becomes the next adder’s Cin. That makes a simple ripple‑carry adder.

Bit‑by‑bit logic

Bit 0: S0 = A0 ⊕ B0 ⊕ Cin, C1 = majority(A0,B0,Cin)

Bit 1: S1 = A1 ⊕ B1 ⊕ C1, Cout = majority(A1,B1,C1)

Binary result

Outputs are Cout S1 S0 — that’s the two‑bit sum, with an extra carry if it overflows.

3) Real‑life analogy (very easy)

Imagine you and a friend are counting coins into stacks of two:

  • A = you put a coin (1) or not (0)
  • B = friend puts a coin (1) or not (0)
  • Cin = there’s already a carry coin from a previous stack (1) or not (0)

If the total coins on the table at this moment are odd (1 or 3), the Sum spot gets a coin (1). If you reach two or more coins, you bundle up two and pass Cout (a carry coin) to the next stack. That’s exactly how the full adder behaves. Two stacks (two full adders) let you count in two bits.

4) Step‑by‑step example

Add A = 10₂ (2) and B = 11₂ (3) with Cin=0.

  1. Bit 0: A0=0, B0=1, Cin=0 → S0 = 1, C1 = 0
  2. Bit 1: A1=1, B1=1, Cin=C1=0 → S1 = 0, Cout = 1

Result = Cout S1 S0 = 1 0 1 = 5 — correct because 2 + 3 = 5.

6) How this maps to your chips

  • 7486 (XOR): makes Sum bits (A ⊕ B ⊕ Cin).
  • 7408 (AND) + 7432 (OR): combine pairs (A·B, A·Cin, B·Cin) to create Carry.
  • Two identical full‑adder blocks chained → your two‑bit adder.
